Theft, Trade and Social Order

Job Market Paper

A model of trade and conflict in which agents choose whether to respect property rights or appropriate goods through coercion.

Abstract | Paper | Slides

What happens in a world without institutions that protect property rights? Hobbes predicted a war of all against all in which the incentive to produce withers. I model a production economy without external protection of property as a class of games in which individuals can trade or seize others’ output by force. Familiar behaviors that govern a market emerge, even when the market is absent. In every subgame perfect equilibrium, individuals allocate productive labor as if jointly optimizing, equalize the return to labor across all goods, and gain nothing from isolation. The threat of theft extends the law of one price beyond markets: an implicit price of power is common to markets, conflicts, and isolated players.

What does not survive is the market’s distribution of payoffs. In every equilibrium, each player’s share of aggregate utility equals her power, her share of the economy’s security, exactly as in universal conflict. Trade occurs only when market shares happen to coincide with power. Even under peaceful trade, every player arms as if she expected to fight. Disarmament and coordination within coalitions could make everyone better off, but some player can always do better still through force.

Perverse Incentives in Policing

Do police commit more crime than everyone else? According to the canonical economic model of crime, they should. Police are tasked with monitoring criminal behavior, potentially reducing the likelihood that their own misconduct is detected and punished. This paper uses experiments to test whether that incentive changes police behavior.

Abstract

Law enforcement and other members of a community that monitor compliance with laws and regulations in a society may face perverse incentives to commit the very crimes that they are supposed to investigate and punish. This paper proposes a series of experiments to test whether those incentives drive behavior among police in America. The experimental results allow us to identify whether police and other monitors require additional monitoring in excess of other members of the community.

Crime and Poverty

In the world we inhabit, poverty and crime are strongly correlated, but the relationship is not causal. This paper shows how the observed correlation can emerge from market forces: comparative advantage jointly determines economic position and incentives for crime. In a counterfactual economy with different technologies, the same mechanism could instead concentrate criminal incentives among the economically advantaged.

Particulate Air Pollution and Daily Mortality on Utah's Wasatch Front

C. Arden Pope III, Randy W. Hill, and G. Martin Villegas

Environmental Health Perspectives, 107(7), 1999, pp. 567–573.

Abstract | Paper

Reviews of daily time-series mortality studies from many cities throughout the world suggest that daily mortality counts are associated with short-term changes in particulate matter (PM) air pollution. One U.S. city, however, with conspicuously weak PM-mortality associations was Salt Lake City, Utah; however, relatively robust PM-mortality associations have been observed in a neighboring metropolitan area (Provo/Orem, Utah). The present study explored this apparent discrepancy by collecting, comparing, and analyzing mortality, pollution, and weather data for all three metropolitan areas on Utah's Wasatch Front region of the Wasatch Mountain Range (Ogden, Salt Lake City, and Provo/Orem) for approximately 10 years (1985-1995). Generalized additive Poisson regression models were used to estimate PM-mortality associations while controlling for seasonality, temperature, humidity, and barometric pressure. Salt Lake City experienced substantially more episodes of high PM that were dominated by windblown dust. When the data were screened to exclude obvious windblown dust episodes and when PM data from multiple monitors were used to construct an estimate of mean exposure for the area, comparable PM-mortality effects were estimated. After screening and by using constructed mean PM [less than/equal to] 10 microm in aerodynamic diameter (PM10) data, the estimated percent change in mortality associated with a 10-mg/m3 increase in PM10 (and 95% confidence intervals) for the three Wasatch Front metropolitan areas equaled approximately 1. 6% (0.3-2.9), 0.8% (0.3-1.3), and 1.0% (0.2-1.8) for the Ogden, Salt Lake City, and Provo/Orem areas, respectively. We conclude that stagnant air pollution episodes with higher concentrations of primary and secondary combustion-source particles were more associated with elevated mortality than windblown dust episodes with relatively higher concentrations of coarse crustal-derived particles.
