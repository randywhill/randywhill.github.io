Theft, Trade and Social Order

Job Market Paper

A model of trade and conflict in which agents choose whether to respect property rights or appropriate goods through coercion.

Abstract | Paper

In the absence of institutions that protect property rights, what conditions allow trade to emerge among self-interested individuals capable of violence? This paper studies a production economy in which individuals can trade peacefully or appropriate goods through conflict. Because conflict, not autarky, is the outside option to trade, peaceful exchange must compete with an alternative that is not equally unattractive to everyone: theft rewards whoever prevails. Trade emerges only when the distribution of economic goods happens to match the distribution of coercive power, and decentralized economic activity provides no mechanism ensuring that alignment.

Organizing into groups could raise returns to specialization in production or coercion, but any division of a group's winnings is itself contested: a member participates only if the group offers more than what she could take by fighting alone. Because coercive power determines each player's claim on cooperative gains, every player has an incentive to expand her own power at the group's expense, undermining the organization specialization would otherwise reward.

A transfer schedule that compensates each player according to her coercive power can, in principle, make peaceful exchange as rewarding as theft. But the existence of such a schedule establishes only the feasibility, not the implementation, of peace: absent an institution to enforce it, each player has an incentive to alter her own coercive power to improve her post-transfer position, unraveling the transfer for the same reason organization unravels. Sustained peace, this paper argues, requires an enforcement mechanism independent of the coercive power it must restrain — a condition decentralized exchange among self-interested individuals cannot supply on its own.

Perverse Incentives in Policing

Do police commit more crime than everyone else? According to the canonical economic model of crime, they should. Police are tasked with monitoring criminal behavior, potentially reducing the likelihood that their own misconduct is detected and punished. This paper uses experiments to test whether that incentive changes police behavior.

Abstract

Law enforcement and other members of a community that monitor compliance with laws and regulations in a society may face perverse incentives to commit the very crimes that they are supposed to investigate and punish. This paper proposes a series of experiments to test whether those incentives drive behavior among police in America. The experimental results allow us to identify whether police and other monitors require additional monitoring in excess of other members of the community

Crime and Poverty

In the world we inhabit, poverty and crime are strongly correlated, but the relationship is not causal. This paper shows how the observed correlation can emerge from market forces: comparative advantage jointly determines economic position and incentives for crime. In a counterfactual economy with different technologies, the same mechanism could instead concentrate criminal incentives among the economically advantaged.
